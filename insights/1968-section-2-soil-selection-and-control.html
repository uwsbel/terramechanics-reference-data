Insights from Section II of the 1968 Bendix report (soil selection and control)

Why these soils

The test soils were chosen to span target ranges set from Surveyor-era estimates of lunar soil: density 20 to 110 pcf, relative density 0 to near 100 percent, friction angle 25 to over 50 degrees, cohesion 0 to under 100 psf, mean grain size under 70 to 2000 microns. The soils were then paired so that one property at a time could be isolated: PS loose against RS loose isolates bulk density (same gradation and friction, very different density); RS loose, intermediate and dense isolate relative density; the SS series does the same for a silica sand. (Table 2-1, printed 2-12, PDF 37; Figure 2-7, printed 2-17, PDF 42.)

The report’s own soil model deliberately includes no strain-rate property: the force on a footpad is written as a function of unit weight, friction, relative density, modulus, cohesion and friction angle only. Rate effects were examined separately in the load-penetration tests and found small up to 4 inches per minute. (printed 2-10, PDF 35; printed 2-74, PDF 99.)

Density and bed preparation

Relative density is defined through the minimum and maximum density test (Burmister funnel method for minimum; vibratory compaction in 1 inch layers for maximum), and unit weight is linear in 1/gamma against Dr, which is why Figure 2-17 is drawn on a reciprocal scale. Acceptance of a determination required values within 3 percent of the average. (printed 2-29 to 2-35, PDF 54 to 60.)

Bed preparation is a first-order effect and the report says so repeatedly. Loose beds (Dr near 0) came from a traveling hopper whose trailing edge rides at the soil surface, designed because screeding a loose surface densifies it; scoop placing gives Dr about 0.15; slope filling segregates coarse and fine grains. Intermediate beds came from screeding thin layers (small LPT bin) or from a lawn roller with a soil-specific number of passes (soil 3: two rolls per layer, Dr about 0.4; soil 8: eight rolls, Dr about 0.6; soil 10: two rolls under cardboard, Dr about 0.5). Dense LPT beds came from a vibratory plate, three passes per 3 inch layer, Dr 0.85 to 0.95; dense beds in the large bins from the roller (soil 4: sixteen rolls, Dr about 0.7; soil 9: twenty-four, Dr 0.75 to 0.8; soil 11: six under cardboard, Dr about 0.7; soil 12: twenty-four, Dr about 0.78). A simulation that claims to reproduce a test must reproduce the stated Dr of that bed, not the nominal state name. (printed 2-103 to 2-113, PDF 128 to 138.)

All soils were tested air dried; moisture 0.2 to 0.3 percent, occasionally 0.6. (printed 2-103, PDF 128.)

The soils degraded during the campaign

The cinder soils (RS, PS, RSM) broke down when handled through the vacuum system used to empty and fill the drag and impact bins; RS and RSM significantly, PS less, SS not at all, and LSM and RC2 were shoveled to protect their fines. Minimum and maximum densities rose with the number of vacuum cycles: for RS from 41.1/53.6 pcf unused to 63.6/76.6 pcf after 46 cycles. Figure 2-60 shows the slope of the density trend increasing after test 1-75, when the vacuum system came into use, while DPT-derived relative density stayed roughly uniform. Consequence: the RS soil in a late drag test is not the RS soil of Table 2-3, and any simulation of a drag or impact test must use the density limits of the bed on that date, which the report tabulates by test number. (printed 2-94 to 2-98, PDF 119 to 123; printed 2-101 to 2-102, PDF 126 to 127.)

Direct shear results and their interpretation

Friction angles of the cinder soils are substantially higher than those of silica sand (29 to 41 degrees for SS against 39 to 53 for the cinders), and their failure envelopes show appreciable apparent cohesion. The authors attribute the high friction to particle angularity and surface roughness, and the apparent cohesion to interlocking or to particle crushing at higher normal stress (their Figure 2-25 hypothesis). They did not use apparent cohesion as a soil descriptor because it did not correlate with static and dynamic strength. (printed 2-45 to 2-52, PDF 70 to 77.)

A footnote gives the number that matters for calibration: a cohesion of about 0.1 psi explains the size effects seen in the load-penetration tests, while the apparent cohesion from direct shear was about 50 times larger. Putting the direct-shear cohesion into Terzaghi’s equation over-predicts bearing strength. (printed 2-47, PDF 72.)

The LSM soil shows no change of friction angle with relative density because the loose LSM densified under the normal load before shearing; its curve in Figure 2-23 is built from the lowest normal loads only. Relative density in the shear tests is the value before the normal load was applied. (printed 2-45 footnote and 2-52, PDF 70 and 77.)

Triaxial tests were few, on RS intermediate and dense only, and gave friction angles of about 38 degrees, or 39 and 43 degrees at low confining pressure and 36 at high when re-examined for crushing. One direct shear series on RS at Dr about 1.0 gave 52.6 degrees against the 49.5 used as the dense RS average. (printed 2-49, PDF 74; printed 2-66 footnote, PDF 91.)

Load-penetration tests

The LPT bin is 18 by 36 by 36 inches with a glass front, on load cells so the bed weight and hence unit weight are known per volume increment. Cylinders of 1, 2, 4 and 8 inch diameter and footpads of 4 and 6.17 inch were pushed at controlled rates to 8 inches or hydraulic capacity. (printed 2-56 to 2-59, PDF 81 to 84.)

Boundary effects were real: in dense SS a failure plane reached the glass face of the 18 inch bin, so tests were repeated in 36 by 36, 54 by 36 and 108 by 36 inch bins. Larger bins gave higher peak loads and lower post-failure slopes; the small bin raised the apparent Nq by about 50 percent for a 1.5 degree change in friction angle. This sets the minimum domain size for a virtual experiment and warns that the 18 inch bin results for dense soils carry a boundary signature. (printed 2-59, 2-68 and 2-74, PDF 84, 93 and 99.)

The dimensionless pressure parameter q/(D gamma) against relative depth Df/D collapses tests of different cylinder size onto one curve for loose and intermediate RS (Dr 0 to 0.7), but not for dense soils, where smaller cylinders read higher; the 1 inch cylinder much higher. The authors conclude that the size effect implied by the bearing capacity equation fails for small cylinders in dense soil. A pronounced dip in one intermediate curve is attributed to screeding as the placement method. (printed 2-69, PDF 94.)

Footpad curves lie below cylinder curves at the same depth; the authors give three reasons: lower average depth of the dished pad, no side friction, and soil falling in above the pad. (printed 2-80, PDF 105.)

The dynamic penetrometer and inferred density

The DPT is a 1.59 lb, 2-3/4 inch aluminum body with a full-radius nose and a 100 g accelerometer, dropped 25.4 inches through a guide tube. Peak deceleration Gp was correlated against directly measured relative density in calibration bins (Figure 2-55) and then used to assign Dr to beds where density could not be measured directly, that is, most drag and impact beds. Two caveats from the authors: calibration bins could not reach Gp values as low as the drag and impact bins did, so the hopper placed soil looser than the minimum density test, or the calibration bins had boundary effects; and in-place density scoop readings scattered and read low. Any validation against a DPT inferred density is therefore conditional on the correlation. (printed 2-88 to 2-94, PDF 113 to 119.)

Bed-to-bed uniformity was good: 49 RS loose beds, four drops each, mean Gp 11.76 plus or minus 0.14 g at 95 percent confidence on the means. (printed 2-90, PDF 115.)

Static penetrometers (a pocket penetrometer and a cone proving-ring penetrometer) were tried and rejected because results varied greatly between operators. There is no static penetrometer data in this report. (printed 2-94, PDF 119.)
